    Understanding Your Due Date: A Calm, Complete Guide

    Finding out you are pregnant often starts with a single, pressing question: when is the baby due? A due date does far more than satisfy curiosity. It anchors your antenatal appointments, times your screening tests and scans, helps you plan work and leave, and gives family and friends a season to look forward to. DueBloom was built to answer that question gently and clearly, using the same standard method that midwives and doctors have used for generations, while also showing you the full picture around the date: how far along you are today in weeks and days, which trimester you are in, how much of the journey is complete, and the key dates in between. This guide explains exactly how the calculation works, why your cycle length matters, what the three trimesters mean in practice, how accurate any estimate can honestly be, and how to work backwards if you already have a due date from a scan.

    How a due date is calculated

    Almost every due date calculator, including this one, starts from the first day of your last menstrual period, often shortened to LMP. That choice can seem odd at first, because conception happens roughly two weeks after that day, but it is used for a practical reason: most people can remember when their last period started far more reliably than they can pinpoint ovulation or conception. From that starting point, pregnancy is counted as lasting 280 days, which is exactly 40 weeks. Adding 280 days to the first day of your last period gives your estimated due date. This counting method also explains a delightful quirk of pregnancy maths: by the time you miss a period and take a test, you are already described as about four weeks pregnant, even though the first two of those weeks happened before conception. Your weeks-and-days figure, such as 12 weeks and 4 days, simply counts every day from that same starting line, which is why DueBloom can show your progress so precisely from just one date.

    Naegele's rule and the calendar method

    The traditional shortcut behind the 280-day method is known as Naegele's rule, named after a German obstetrician. It says to take the first day of your last period, add one year, subtract three months, and add seven days. Try it on a calendar and you will land on almost exactly the same date as adding 280 days, with tiny differences caused only by months having different lengths. DueBloom uses direct day counting rather than month arithmetic, so your result never wobbles because February is short or because a month has 31 days instead of 30. The gentle calendar on this page then shows your due month with the big day marked, because seeing the date sitting among ordinary Tuesdays and weekends somehow makes it feel real in a way that a bare number never quite does. Both approaches rest on the same assumption, though: a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. If that is not your pattern, the next section matters a lot for you.

    Why cycle length changes everything

    The standard calculation quietly assumes ovulation happens on day 14 of a 28-day cycle. In reality, cycles commonly run from about 21 to 35 days, and the part of the cycle that stretches or shrinks is almost always the part before ovulation. The phase after ovulation, called the luteal phase, stays fairly steady at around 14 days for most people. That means someone with a regular 35-day cycle usually ovulates near day 21, a full week later than the textbook assumption, so conception happens later and the baby genuinely needs those extra days. DueBloom handles this with a simple, honest adjustment: for every day your cycle is longer than 28, your due date moves one day later, and for every day it is shorter, it moves one day earlier. A 32-day cycle therefore adds four days, while a 24-day cycle subtracts four. Use the slider to set the length that is usual for you, ideally based on several recent cycles rather than a single unusual month. If your cycles are very irregular, treat any calculator result as a rough guide and lean on your dating scan instead.

    The three trimesters, week by week in spirit

    Pregnancy is traditionally divided into three trimesters, and the timeline on this page highlights the one you are in. The first trimester runs from week 1 to the end of week 13. These are the foundation weeks: the fertilised egg implants, the neural tube forms, and tiny buds that will become arms and legs appear. Many people feel their strongest tiredness and nausea in this stretch, even though there may be little to see from the outside. The second trimester, weeks 14 to 27, is often described as the most comfortable phase. Energy frequently returns, the nausea fades for many, you will usually feel the first fluttering movements, and the halfway point at 20 weeks brings the detailed anatomy scan. The third trimester, from week 28 until birth, is the finishing stretch: the baby gains weight rapidly, practices breathing movements, and settles into position, while you prepare practically and emotionally for labour. Knowing your trimester is more than trivia. Screening tests, vaccinations, glucose testing, and growth scans are all scheduled by trimester and week, so the number genuinely organises your care.

    Weeks and days: reading your progress like a midwife

    Healthcare professionals rarely talk about months of pregnancy, because months are fuzzy: a month can be 28 or 31 days, and being five months pregnant could mean anything from about 20 to 23 weeks. Weeks and days are exact, which is why DueBloom leads with them. When your result says 18 weeks and 3 days, it means 129 days have passed since the first day of your last period. The progress bar translates that into a percentage of a typical 280-day pregnancy, so at 140 days you are exactly halfway. This precision matters at the edges of pregnancy too. A baby born before 37 completed weeks is described as preterm, 37 to 38 weeks is early term, 39 to 40 is full term, 41 is late term, and 42 weeks and beyond is post-term. Those labels influence real decisions about monitoring and induction, and they all hinge on accurate dating, which is one more reason to bring your calculated dates to your first appointment and compare them with your scan.

    How accurate is any due date, honestly?

    It helps to hold your due date lightly. Only about one baby in twenty arrives on the exact predicted day, and the large majority of healthy, spontaneous births happen somewhere between 37 and 42 weeks. Think of the date less as a deadline and more as the centre of a likely window, with the two weeks on either side being entirely normal territory. Several things nudge the real day around: natural variation in ovulation even in regular cycles, uncertainty about the exact period date, and the simple fact that babies grow at slightly different rates. An ultrasound in the first trimester, ideally between about 8 and 14 weeks, measures the baby directly and is generally considered the most accurate dating method, which is why providers sometimes adjust your date after that scan. If that happens, do not worry that you did anything wrong; the calculator gave the best estimate possible from periods alone, and the scan simply refined it. Use reverse mode in DueBloom to adopt the scan date as your new anchor.

    Working backwards from a known due date

    Sometimes the due date comes first. Perhaps a scan report gave you a date, a provider wrote one on your notes, or you conceived through fertility treatment with precisely known timings. Reverse mode exists for exactly that situation. Enter the due date you trust, and DueBloom subtracts 280 days to estimate the first day of your last period, and 266 days to estimate conception, then shows how far along you are today and how many days remain. This is genuinely useful: it lets you sanity-check paperwork, reconstruct dates for forms that ask about your last period, and understand your timeline if your periods were irregular or absent before conception. Remember that the reconstructed period date is a mathematical equivalent rather than a guaranteed historical fact, especially if your scan date was itself adjusted for a longer or shorter cycle, but as a consistent reference frame for planning, it works beautifully.

    What to do with your dates next

    Once you have your estimate, a few gentle next steps help. Book your first antenatal appointment if you have not already, since booking windows and the first screening tests are time-sensitive. Note your key dates: conception estimate, the end of your first trimester, the end of your second, and of course the due date itself, all of which DueBloom lists for copying or printing. Start folic acid if your provider advises it and you are not already taking it, as it matters most in the earliest weeks. Beyond the practical list, allow yourself the human side of the number too: telling loved ones a season, imagining the weather on the birthday, and watching the progress bar creep forward week by week. And if anything worries you at any point, such as pain, bleeding, or simply not knowing your dates at all, contact your doctor, midwife, or local early pregnancy service promptly rather than relying on any website. DueBloom gives you a clear, carefully calculated starting point; your care team turns it into a plan made just for you.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is my due date calculated?

    DueBloom adds 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last menstrual period, then adjusts for your cycle length. A 28-day cycle needs no adjustment, a longer cycle pushes the date later, and a shorter cycle brings it earlier.

    What if my cycle is longer or shorter than 28 days?

    Use the cycle-length slider to set your usual cycle between 21 and 35 days. DueBloom adds one day to the due date for every day your cycle is longer than 28, and subtracts one day for every day it is shorter, because ovulation timing shifts with cycle length.

    How accurate is a due date calculator?

    A due date is an estimate, not a deadline. Only about 1 in 20 babies arrive on the exact predicted day, and most healthy births happen between 37 and 42 weeks. An early ultrasound scan is usually the most accurate way to confirm your dates.

    What does weeks and days mean, like 12 weeks 4 days?

    Pregnancy age is counted in completed weeks plus extra days since your last period. 12 weeks 4 days means 88 days have passed. DueBloom shows this weeks-and-days figure, your current trimester, and what percentage of a typical 280-day pregnancy is complete.

    Can I work backwards from a due date I already have?

    Yes. Switch DueBloom to reverse mode, enter the due date from your scan or provider, and it will estimate your last period date, your likely conception date, how far along you are today, and how many days remain until your due date.

    When should I talk to my doctor or midwife about my dates?

    Share your calculated dates at your first antenatal appointment. Talk to your provider sooner if your periods are very irregular, you recently stopped hormonal contraception, you are unsure of your last period date, or you have any pain or bleeding.
