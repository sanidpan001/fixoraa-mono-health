The CaloFuel Guide

TDEE, BMR & Calories: The Complete Fuel Manual

Every fitness goal — losing fat, building muscle, or simply maintaining the body you have — runs on one number: your daily calorie target. Get it right and the rest of your plan (training, sleep, protein) falls into place. Get it wrong and even perfect workouts feel like pushing a car uphill with the handbrake on. This guide explains exactly how calorie calculators work, what the three major BMR formulas do differently, and how to turn your numbers into a realistic plan with a real deadline.

What BMR and TDEE Actually Mean

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the energy your body burns in 24 hours doing absolutely nothing — lying still, awake, in a warm room. It powers your brain, heart, lungs, digestion and the constant repair work happening inside your cells. For most adults, BMR accounts for roughly 60 to 75 percent of all calories burned in a day, which is why every serious calorie calculator starts there.

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is BMR plus everything else: walking to the train, fidgeting at your desk, workouts, and even the small energy cost of digesting food (the thermic effect of food, about 10 percent of intake). The formula is simple: TDEE = BMR × activity multiplier. Eat at your TDEE and your weight stays roughly stable. Eat below it and you lose weight. Eat above it and you gain. That single relationship is the entire science of weight management — everything else is execution.

The Three BMR Formulas Compared

CaloFuel lets you choose between three peer-reviewed equations, because no single formula is perfect for every body. Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is the modern default and the most validated for the general population: research found it predicts resting energy expenditure within 10 percent for about 82 percent of people. Men: 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age + 5. Women: the same, minus 161 instead of plus 5.

Harris-Benedict, revised in 1984, is the classic equation many older calculators still use. It tends to overestimate by around 5 percent for modern, mostly sedentary populations, which makes it a useful upper-bound cross-check. Men: 88.362 + 13.397 × weight + 4.799 × height − 5.677 × age. Women: 447.593 + 9.247 × weight + 3.098 × height − 4.330 × age.

Katch-McArdle ignores sex entirely and works from lean body mass: 370 + 21.6 × lean mass (kg). It is the most accurate option if you genuinely know your body-fat percentage from a DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or a decent body-composition scale. Guess your body fat badly and Katch-McArdle becomes worse than Mifflin-St Jeor — which is why CaloFuel refuses to show you a Katch-McArdle number from a missing input, and falls back to Mifflin-St Jeor with a clear on-screen note.

A quick worked example shows how close the formulas run for ordinary people. Take a 30-year-old man, 70 kg, 175 cm, 20 percent body fat. Mifflin-St Jeor gives about 1,649 kcal/day. Harris-Benedict (revised) gives about 1,696. Katch-McArdle gives about 1,580. That spread of roughly 115 kcal is normal — and it is exactly why the honest answer to "which is most accurate" is: the one whose assumptions match your body, cross-checked against the others.

Activity Multipliers: Pick the Life That Looks Like Yours

The biggest error in calorie calculation is not the formula — it is the activity level. Most people choose one level too high. CaloFuel uses the standard FAO/WHO multipliers and attaches concrete examples to each label so you cannot fool yourself accidentally. Sedentary (×1.2) means a desk job like IT, banking, or accounting with little or no exercise. Lightly active (×1.375) adds one to three easy sessions a week — walks, gentle yoga. Moderately active (×1.55) covers on-your-feet jobs such as teaching, nursing, or retail, plus three to five real workouts a week. Very active (×1.725) means physical labor like construction or farming, or six to seven hard training sessions weekly. Extremely active (×1.9) is reserved for professional athletes and twice-daily training — boxers, elite runners, soldiers in training.

When in doubt, pick the lower option for two weeks, watch the scale trend, and adjust. A calculator gives you a starting line, not a verdict: the real TDEE is the one your bodyweight trend confirms. Weigh yourself at the same time each morning and judge by the two-week average, never a single day.

Setting Your Deficit or Surplus: The 7,700 Rule

One kilogram of body fat stores roughly 7,700 kilocalories. That single constant turns weight goals into daily math. Want to lose half a kilogram a week? You need a deficit of 0.5 × 7,700 ÷ 7 = 550 kcal per day. Want to gain a quarter kilo of mostly muscle a week? A surplus of about 275 kcal per day. CaloFuel's goal-date planner runs this math in reverse: tell it your target weight and date, and it computes the exact daily deficit or surplus required, the weekly pace that implies, and whether that pace is safe.

Safe pace has evidence-based guardrails. For fat loss, most sports-nutrition guidelines cap sustainable loss at about 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight per week (roughly 0.5–1 kg/week for most adults). Faster than that and you start losing meaningful muscle, tanking energy, and inviting the rebound. For muscle gain, a surplus of 250–500 kcal/day is the sweet spot; more mostly becomes fat. And there are absolute floors: going below about 1,200 kcal/day (women) or 1,500 kcal/day (men) without medical supervision risks nutrient deficiencies and metabolic adaptation. CaloFuel's planner flags all three situations — aggressive pace, extreme surplus, and unsafe floors — with plain-language warnings, not silent approval.

Why a Goal-Date Planner Beats a Static Target

Most calculators hand you one number — "eat 2,050 kcal" — with no context for when you will arrive. A goal-date planner changes the psychology: a wedding in 12 weeks or a marathon in 20 weeks becomes a required daily pace, and the feasibility warning becomes your honesty check. If the math says you need a 1,200 kcal daily deficit to hit your date, the tool tells you that timeline is unrealistic rather than letting you starve for three weeks and quit. Two practical rules make planners work. First, as you lose weight your BMR and TDEE fall, so the same calorie target produces a smaller deficit over time — re-run your numbers every two to four weeks. Second, build in a two-week buffer: life happens, plateaus happen, and a plan that only works with zero missed days is a plan designed to fail.

Macro Splits: Protein, Carbs, and Fat in Grams

Calories determine whether your weight moves; macros determine what your body is made of when it does. Protein (4 kcal/g) builds and preserves muscle — aim for roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight when cutting, slightly less when maintaining. Carbohydrates (4 kcal/g) are your training fuel. Fat (9 kcal/g) supports hormones and satiety; dropping below about 0.5 g/kg for long stretches is asking for trouble.

CaloFuel ships three presets. Balanced (25% protein / 50% carbs / 25% fat) suits general active people and most gym-goers. High-protein (35/35/30) is the cutting preset: extra protein protects muscle in a deficit and keeps hunger down. Keto (25/5/70) mirrors the classic ketogenic split for people who train their bodies to run on fat. Whatever preset you pick, the grams matter more than the percentages — grams are what you weigh on a food scale.

Five Mistakes That Wreck Good Numbers

One: counting exercise twice. If your tracker adds workout calories on top of a "very active" multiplier, you are double-counting. Pick the activity level that already includes your training. Two: weighing food by eye. Studies show people under-report intake by 20–40 percent; a 30-dollar kitchen scale beats any app. Three: chasing daily scale noise. Water, glycogen, and salt swing weight by a kilogram overnight — judge the two-week trend. Four: slashing calories the moment progress stalls. Plateaus of one to two weeks are normal; hold the target, check sleep and steps, then adjust by 100–200 kcal. Five: ignoring protein. The most common reason a cut ends with a smaller but still-soft physique is protein far below 1.6 g/kg. Fix that before you touch calories.

A Sample Day at the Numbers

Put it together for our example athlete: 30-year-old man, 70 kg, 175 cm, moderately active. BMR (Mifflin-St Jeor) ≈ 1,649. TDEE ≈ 1,649 × 1.55 ≈ 2,556 kcal. Goal: reach 65 kg in 10 weeks (70 days). Required daily deficit: 5 × 7,700 ÷ 70 = 550 kcal/day, a pace of 0.5 kg/week — inside the safe zone, so no warnings. Daily target: ≈ 2,006 kcal. Balanced macros: ≈ 125 g protein, ≈ 251 g carbs, ≈ 56 g fat. That is the entire plan on an index card — and it is exactly what CaloFuel's copyable summary gives you, formatted for your notes or your coach.

Remember the fine print that matters: these are estimates for healthy adults. If you are under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, managing a medical condition, or recovering from an eating disorder, work with a qualified professional instead of a calculator. For everyone else, the formula is unglamorous and undefeated — measure honestly, eat to the target, train hard, sleep well, and let the weeks do the work.
