Your weekly split

What a workout split actually is

A workout split is simply how you divide training across the week. The split matters less than beginners think and more than advanced lifters admit: what matters most is that every muscle gets trained, recovered, and trained again. A good split organises that rhythm around your real life — the number of days you can actually train, consistently, for months.

GymSplit starts from the only input that really constrains the answer: days per week. Three days gives you the classic push/pull/legs. Four days gives you upper/lower twice through. Six days gives you push/pull/legs twice. The "best" split is the one you'll still be doing in six months.

The big three split families

Push / Pull / Legs

Push day trains chest, shoulders and triceps (bench press, overhead press, dips). Pull day trains back and biceps (rows, pull-ups, curls). Leg day trains quads, hamstrings and glutes (squats, deadlifts, lunges). PPL is the most popular split in existence because the logic is airtight: muscles that work together recover together. Run it once per week on 3 days, or twice on 6 days for higher frequency.

Upper / Lower

Upper day hits everything above the waist; lower day everything below. On 2 days it's the minimum effective program. On 4 days (upper, lower, upper, lower) it's arguably the best balance of frequency and recovery ever devised — each muscle gets trained twice weekly with 48–72 hours between sessions, which is the sweet spot research keeps confirming.

Bro split (one muscle per day)

Chest day, back day, leg day, shoulder day, arm day — the classic bodybuilder week. It works, but each muscle waits a full week between sessions, which is suboptimal for natural lifters. GymSplit uses it only as a component (inside 5-day plans) rather than a pure 5-day bro split, so frequency stays higher.

Matching the plan to your goal

Goal: build muscle (hypertrophy)

Train each muscle 2× per week, 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, mostly 6–12 reps, resting 60–90 seconds. Exercise selection favours movements you can progress and feel: dumbbell presses, rows, Romanian deadlifts, leg presses. The last 1–2 reps of each set should be genuinely hard.

Goal: get stronger

Centre the plan on heavy compounds — squat, bench, deadlift, overhead press — in the 3–6 rep range with 3–5 minutes rest. Assistance work stays lighter. Strength is a skill; practising the big lifts frequently (the 4-day upper/lower is ideal) with patient 2.5–5 lb jumps is the entire game.

Goal: general fitness

Mix it up: compounds for strength, machines and dumbbells for muscle, and finishers or conditioning for the heart. Rep ranges 8–12, rest 60–90 seconds, and include something you enjoy — adherence is the program.

Exercise selection rules

Every session needs: one primary compound (the heavy lift of the day), 2–3 secondary compounds or machines, and 1–2 isolation finishers. That's 4–6 exercises per session — more is junk volume for most people. Pick exercises your joints tolerate; the "best" exercise you can't do pain-free is never the best exercise for you. Rest days are training days

Muscle is built in recovery, not in the gym — training is the stimulus, rest is the adaptation. Schedule at least one full rest day per week (two on 5–6 day plans), sleep 7–9 hours, and eat enough protein (roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight daily). Soreness is not a progress metric; performance trending upward over weeks is.

When to change your split

Run a split for at least 8–12 weeks before judging it. Change when: progress stalls for 3+ weeks despite good sleep and food, your schedule changes, you're bored enough that compliance is slipping, or nagging joint pain suggests a movement pattern needs rotating. Change the split — don't program-hop weekly. Consistency with a good plan beats novelty every time.

Sample week: the 3-day push/pull/legs

Monday — Push: barbell bench press 4×6–8, overhead press 3×8–10, incline dumbbell press 3×10–12, lateral raises 3×12–15, triceps pushdowns 3×10–12. Wednesday — Pull: deadlift 3×5, pull-ups 4×max, barbell row 4×8–10, face pulls 3×12–15, barbell curls 3×10–12. Friday — Legs: back squat 4×6–8, Romanian deadlift 3×8–10, leg press 3×10–12, walking lunges 3×12 per leg, standing calf raises 4×12–15. Rest days between sessions let each muscle group recover fully — this is the highest-compliance starter program in existence for a reason.

Sample week: the 4-day upper/lower

Monday — Upper (strength): bench 5×5, Pendlay row 5×5, overhead press 3×6–8, weighted pull-ups 3×6–8. Tuesday — Lower (strength): squat 5×5, Romanian deadlift 3×6–8, Bulgarian split squats 3×8–10. Thursday — Upper (volume): incline dumbbell press 4×8–12, lat pulldown 4×10–12, lateral raises 3×12–15, hammer curls + pushdowns 3×10–12. Friday — Lower (volume): front squat 4×8–10, leg press 3×12–15, walking lunges 3×12, calf raises 4×15. Heavy days build the strength base; volume days build the muscle — the combination is why intermediates thrive on this split.

Deload weeks: the scheduled step back

Every 6–8 weeks, take a deload: cut volume roughly in half (same exercises, ~50% of the sets, lighter loads) for one week. You'll feel like you're slacking — that's the point. Fatigue masks fitness; the deload dissipates accumulated fatigue and you return stronger. Lifters who skip deloads don't avoid them; they take them involuntarily as injuries or burnout. Schedule it like a training day.

Where cardio fits

Cardio doesn't kill gains — badly timed cardio does. Keep hard conditioning away from leg day (24+ hours of separation), favour low-impact options (cycling, incline walking, rowing) during heavy strength blocks, and cap intense sessions at 2 per week. Two to three easy 20–30 minute sessions actually improve recovery by increasing blood flow. Your heart is a muscle too; train it deliberately rather than guiltily.

Adjusting when life happens

Missed a day? Don't stack two sessions into one mega-workout — just slide the week forward or drop the least important session (isolation work goes first, compounds stay). Travelling? One full-body dumbbell session per week maintains nearly all your progress for up to three weeks. The split is a template, not a religion: GymSplit regenerates around your new schedule in seconds, which is exactly what it's for.

Tracking progressive overload

The program only works if the weights go up over time. Log every session's top set (weight × reps) in any notes app, and review monthly: a 2.5–5% monthly climb on your main lifts means the split is working. When progress stalls for three weeks despite good sleep and food, change one variable — add a set, swap an exercise, or take a deload week — not the whole program. Small, patient adjustments beat program-hopping every time.

The warm-up protocol: ramp to your working weight

Jumping straight to working weight is the fastest route to a strained shoulder and a wasted first set. A proper ramp-up wakes up the nervous system, rehearses the movement pattern and tells you how today's strength feels — all in about ten minutes.

The protocol: take your working weight and do four to five warm-up sets with climbing load and falling reps, resting 60–90 seconds between them. Worked example for a 100 kg squat day:

  • Bar (20 kg) × 10 — groove the pattern, deep and controlled.
  • 40 kg × 8 — easy, find your rhythm.
  • 60 kg × 5 — moderate, brace properly.
  • 80 kg × 3 — heavy-ish, rehearse the full setup.
  • 90 kg × 1 — feel what today's strength is.

Then start your 100 kg working sets. Notice the reps fall as the weight climbs: warm-ups are rehearsal, not training, and should never fatigue you. The single at ~90% is the key set — it calibrates your body so the first work set never feels like a shock. For upper-body lifts, three to four ramp sets are enough; for deadlifts, add one extra single.

Rule of thumb: you should break a light sweat but still feel fresh. If your warm-up leaves you winded, you did too much.

Five mistakes that stall every beginner

Most first-year plateaus aren't genetic — they're these five habits:

  • Program hopping. Switching plans every three weeks means you never accumulate enough practice on any lift to progress. Pick a split and commit to twelve weeks.
  • Adding weight before owning the form. A heavier squat with collapsing knees is not progress; it's a future injury. Film your main lifts monthly and fix what you see before loading more.
  • Training to failure on everything. Grinding every set to the last possible rep wrecks recovery. Leave 1–2 reps in reserve on most sets; go all-out only on the final set of an isolation move.
  • Ignoring protein and sleep. Muscle is built from protein and consolidated during sleep. Around 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight and 7–9 hours of sleep matter more than any supplement.
  • Skipping legs. Half your muscle mass sits below the waist. A split that trains legs properly builds your whole body faster — squats and deadlifts drive systemic growth.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best workout split for 4 days a week?

Three days: push/pull/legs. Four days: upper/lower/upper/lower — arguably the best all-round split. Five or six days only if you can sustain the schedule at 90%+ compliance for months.

What muscles do push pull legs train?

Push day = chest, shoulders, triceps (bench, overhead press). Pull day = back, biceps (rows, pull-ups). Leg day = quads, hamstrings, glutes (squats, deadlifts). Muscles that work together recover together.

Is training 2 days a week enough to build muscle?

No — consistency beats frequency. Two full-body or upper/lower days done every week outperforms four days done sporadically. Pick the day count you'll actually sustain, then progress from there.

How many reps and sets per muscle group?

For muscle: 6–12 reps, 10–20 sets per muscle weekly. For strength: 3–6 reps on heavy compounds with longer rests. For general fitness: 8–12 reps with 60–90 second rests.

How long should I stick with one workout split?

At least 8–12 weeks. Changing programs weekly prevents the progressive overload that drives growth. Switch only when progress stalls for 3+ weeks, your schedule changes, or compliance is slipping.

Why are rest days important for muscle growth?

Muscle grows during recovery, not during the workout. Take 1–2 full rest days weekly, sleep 7–9 hours, and eat ~1.6–2.2g protein per kg bodyweight. Soreness isn't progress — rising performance over weeks is.
